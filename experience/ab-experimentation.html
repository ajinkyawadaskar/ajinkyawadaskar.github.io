A/B Experimentation Framework

How I designed and deployed an end-to-end experimentation system with causal inference at Ford Motor Company.

// the framework

Ford's digital products needed a systematic way to test changes and measure their real impact — not just "did the metric go up?" but "did the change cause the metric to go up?" The difference matters when you're making resource allocation decisions on the result.

I designed an end-to-end experimentation pipeline:

  • Hypothesis framing — structured templates so product teams state what they expect, what metric they're moving, and what would make them stop the experiment
  • Power analysis — sample size calculation before running, not after. How long does the experiment need to run to detect the expected effect size?
  • Experiment execution — instrumented via Amplitude, results piped into BigQuery
  • Diff-in-diff causal lift estimation — the core analytical method
  • Guardrail metrics — ensuring the experiment doesn't degrade secondary KPIs while optimizing the primary one
  • Anomaly detection — catching instrumentation failures and sample ratio mismatches mid-flight, not after the experiment ends
// why diff-in-diff

A standard A/B test assumes you can randomly assign users to treatment and control. In practice, Ford's digital products had pre-existing user cohorts and regional rollout constraints that made full randomization impractical for some experiments.

Diff-in-diff accounts for this by comparing the change in the treatment group against the change in the control group over the same period. It isolates the causal effect of the intervention from baseline drift — the trend that would have happened anyway. The assumption is that both groups would have followed the same trend absent the treatment (parallel trends), which is testable against pre-treatment data.

This made the 4% conversion lift a defensible causal claim rather than a before-after comparison that could have been driven by seasonality, a concurrent marketing campaign, or organic growth.

// what made it work in practice

The statistical method was the easy part. What made the framework actually useful in a large organization:

  • Power analysis upfront — before every experiment, not a retroactive justification. Product teams knew how long to wait before looking at results, which prevented early peeking and false positives.
  • Guardrail metrics — every experiment had a primary metric it was trying to move and 2-3 guardrails it was not allowed to degrade. This prevented optimizing one metric at the expense of another.
  • SRM diagnostics — sample ratio mismatch detection caught instrumentation bugs that would have silently corrupted results. Two experiments were flagged and paused before bad data could influence a decision.
  • Standardized reporting — results went to stakeholders in a consistent format: effect size, confidence interval, guardrail status, and a clear recommendation. No ambiguity about what the experiment showed.

Result: 4% measured conversion lift, with the causal claim defensible under scrutiny.
